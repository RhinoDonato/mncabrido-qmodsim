01 · MODULES 1 & 3

Device Arrivals

Drop-offs are represented as random events over an eight-hour business day. The usual arrival rate forms a steady baseline; a smooth, time-varying surge raises that rate around the selected rush hour.

λ(t) = λbase + A · e−(t−tₚ)² / 2σ²

The chart shows the expected arrival rate at each time. A Poisson sample gives one possible day, so the sampled total changes between runs even when the settings stay fixed. The expected daily count is the area under the rate curve.

Assumptions and limits

Arrivals are independent, the surge width stays fixed, and the rate curve describes the day. Real customers may arrive in groups or respond to events the model does not include.

02 · MODULE 6

Technician Queue

The M/M/c/K model tracks a workshop with random arrivals, c technicians, and a maximum of K devices in the shop. When the system is full, another arrival is turned away.

ρ = λ / (cμ)   ·   W = L / λeff

Utilization ρ compares arrivals λ with the combined repair capacity cμ. The model calculates the long-run chance of each occupancy, the chance the shop is full, expected queue time, and technician use.

Assumptions and limits

Arrival and repair times are memoryless, technicians have the same average repair rate, and the model is at steady state. It does not represent repair complexity or jobs that take different amounts of time.

03 · MODULE 5

Job Order Journey

A Markov chain models the chance a job moves from its current state to another: diagnosis, quote, parts, repair, ready, or warranty. Paid collection and abandonment end the journey.

N = (I − Q)−1   ·   B = NR

Q contains transitions among active stages. The fundamental matrix N estimates expected visits before an absorbing outcome; B estimates the eventual outcome probabilities. Adjust quote approval, parts availability, pickup, and deposit assumptions in the Lab.

Assumptions and limits

Next-step chances depend only on the current state. The model uses fixed transition probabilities and a simplified warranty loop, rather than calendar time or individual job histories.

04 · MODULE 4

Annual Risk

Monte Carlo simulation generates 100,000 possible years. In each one, event frequency, the chance an event succeeds, and the financial severity are sampled from the selected ranges.

ALE ≈ (1 / N) Σ lossi

The expected annual loss is the average across sampled years. The 95th percentile is a high-loss threshold: about five percent of simulated years exceed it. It is not a worst-case estimate.

Assumptions and limits

Loss events are represented with simplified PERT, beta, and lognormal distributions. Results depend strongly on those inputs and should not be treated as observed business data or a prediction.

05 · MODULE 7

Deposit Strategy

A payoff game compares three shop policies—no deposit, partial deposit, and full parts payment—with customer choices to approve, abandon after ordering, or cancel mid-repair.

Expected payoff = Σ P(choice) × payoff

The displayed strategy mix is a Nash equilibrium for the payoff table: neither player can improve their modelled outcome by changing strategy alone. It helps compare trade-offs when parts costs, margin, and deposits change.

Assumptions and limits

Payoffs are simplified estimates and customer responses are represented by the game. The equilibrium is not evidence of what a real customer or shop will choose.

06 · MODULES 2 & 10

Tracking ID Security

Entropy measures how unpredictable a token is. A sequential job number can reveal neighboring records; a randomly generated reference has many more possible combinations.

H = L · log₂(|Σ|)

For a token of length L chosen from an alphabet Σ, the model calculates the entropy in bits and average guessing time at the chosen guess rate. A rate limit extends that time.

Assumptions and limits

Characters are assumed to be selected uniformly and independently. Random IDs should still be paired with authorization checks, rate limits, and careful handling of customer information.

07 · MODULE 12

System Reliability

In a series workflow, every required service must be available. A backup notification path raises communication availability; an offline intake cache can reduce dependence on the network.

Aseries = A₁ × A₂ × ··· × Aₙ

Availability is the long-run fraction of time a component works. Expected yearly downtime is 8,760 hours times the chance the overall workflow is unavailable.

Assumptions and limits

Component failures are independent, average repair times apply consistently, and failover works immediately. Correlated outages and real recovery delays are not included.

01 Device Arrivals · Modules 1 & 3

Problem. Estimate device drop-offs during ordinary hours and payday/weekend surges.

λ(t) = λ₀ + A · exp(−(t−tₚ)²/(2σ²)); E[N(T)] = ∫₀ᵀ λ(t)dt

Symbols and units
λ₀ and A: devices/hour; t and tₚ: hours after opening; σ: hours; N(T): devices.
Derivation
Start with a homogeneous Poisson rate λ₀, add a Gaussian-shaped surge, then numerically integrate its area. Exponential inter-arrival samples use −ln(U)/λ.
Worked example
With λ₀=6/h, A=18/h, tₚ=4.5 h, and σ=.72 h, the peak rate is 24 devices/hour; the Lab integrates the eight-hour total.
Interpretation
The integral is a long-run mean; one sampled day varies around it.
Assumptions and limitations
Independent arrivals and a fixed surge shape; real group arrivals and weather/events can violate these assumptions.

02 Technician Queue · Module 6

Problem. Quantify workshop occupancy, turn-away, waiting, and total time with finite shelves.

ρ = λ/(cμ); λeff = λ(1−Pᴋ); Wq = Lq/λeff; W = L/λeff

Symbols and units
λ: devices/hour; μ: repairs/technician/hour; c: technicians; K: total device capacity; Wq and W: hours.
Derivation
Assign birth–death weights (λ/μ)ⁿ/n! below c and the c-server geometric tail above c, normalize to probabilities, then apply Little’s Law.
Worked example
λ=5/h, μ=1.5/h, c=3, K=12. The engine calculates Pᴋ, effective arrivals, Lq, Wq, and W without confusing wait time with total system time.
Interpretation
High utilization can coexist with low blocking when K is large, but waiting may still grow.
Assumptions and limitations
Poisson arrivals, exponential equal-rate service, steady state, and K≥c.

03 Job Order Journey · Module 5

Problem. Estimate paid versus abandoned outcomes and lifecycle steps across nine repair states.

P = [Q R; 0 I]; N = (I−Q)⁻¹; t = N·1; B = N·R

Symbols and units
P, Q, R, N, B: dimensionless probability matrices; t: state-transition steps.
Derivation
Reorder seven transient and two absorbing states into canonical form. Invert I−Q; use the Intake row of t for a job beginning at S0 and the Intake row of B for final outcomes.
Worked example
Approval=.78, parts=.65, pickup=.86, deposit=.30. The Lab displays all rows and verifies every outgoing sum equals one.
Interpretation
Expected steps are transitions, not clock hours; warranty S8 can loop to repair before absorption.
Assumptions and limitations
Memoryless, fixed next-state probabilities; calendar delays and customer heterogeneity are excluded.

04 FAIR Annual Risk · Module 4

Problem. Quantify illustrative annual loss from customer-data exposure, abandoned parts, and inventory slippage.

TEF ~ Beta-PERT(low, mode, high); LM ~ Lognormal(μ,σ); ALE = mean annual loss; ROSI = ((ALE₀−ALE₁)−C)/C

Symbols and units
TEF: events/year; vulnerability: probability; LM, ALE, C: Philippine pesos; VaR₉₅: pesos at the 95th percentile.
Derivation
Sample TEF and vulnerability, draw a Poisson event count, sum lognormal severities for each trial, then sort annual totals for VaR₉₅.
Worked example
PERT(2,6,20), vulnerability .35, median loss ₱25,000, σ=.65, 100,000 trials. Seeded verification runs use 10,000 trials.
Interpretation
ALE is an average, while VaR₉₅ marks a tail threshold—not a maximum.
Assumptions and limitations
All financial inputs are illustrative unless replaced with measured shop evidence; stochastic estimates can differ by seed.

05 Deposit Strategy · Module 7

Problem. Balance non-returnable parts exposure against customer friction.

E[U] = Σ pⱼ Uⱼ; equilibrium requires no profitable unilateral deviation

Symbols and units
U: peso-valued utility; pⱼ: customer-choice probability; deposit: fraction of parts cost.
Derivation
Build a 3×3 normal-form game for shop policy versus customer action, solve equal-payoff supports, then reject any candidate with a profitable deviation.
Worked example
Part ₱6,000, margin ₱3,000, labor ₱1,000, friction .04, customer value ₱25,000.
Interpretation
A verified model equilibrium applies only to this payoff table; it is not proof of real behavior or the PDF’s proposed linear-program optimization.
Assumptions and limitations
Utilities and response probabilities are illustrative and omit repeat-business effects.

06 Tracking ID Security · Modules 2 & 10

Problem. Reduce enumeration risk against public job-order lookups.

H = L · log₂(|Σ|); S = |Σ|ᴸ; T = S/(2R)

Symbols and units
H: bits; L: characters; |Σ|: alphabet size; S: identifiers; R: guesses/second; T: seconds.
Derivation
Uniform independent characters create S equiprobable tokens, so entropy is log₂(S). Average exhaustive search tests half the space.
Worked example
A four-digit space has about 13.3 bits; a 12-character base-62 token has about 71.4 bits. Rate reduction from 100/s to .05/s adds a 2,000× time factor.
Interpretation
The Lab uses Web Crypto for sample tokens. Random IDs are defense in depth, not authorization.
Assumptions and limitations
The time estimate assumes uniform generation and a fixed request rate. Real controls also need object-level authorization, rate limits, and audit logs.

07 System Reliability · Module 12

Problem. Estimate availability of terminal, API, ledger, and redundant notifications.

A = MTBF/(MTBF+MTTR); Aparallel = 1−Π(1−Aᵢ); Asystem = Π Aᵢ

Symbols and units
MTBF and MTTR: hours; A: dimensionless availability; downtime: hours/year.
Derivation
Calculate each component’s inherent availability, combine SMS and email in parallel, multiply required series links, then use 8,760(1−A).
Worked example
Terminal MTBF 4,000 h, API 3,500 h, ledger 5,000 h, MTTR 4 h, SMS .995, email .992.
Interpretation
The offline-intake figure is a theoretical scenario only; this artifact does not claim an offline-capable customer-record system.
Assumptions and limitations
Independent failures, immediate failover, stable rates, and no correlated ISP/power outage.

Legal & Engineering Crosswalk

Customer data protection and RA 10173

Legal reference: Republic Act No. 10173 requires personal-information processing to follow transparency, legitimate purpose, and proportionality, and Section 20 requires reasonable and appropriate organizational, physical, and technical safeguards. The law also addresses data-subject rights and accountability. Read the National Privacy Commission text and Lawphil copy.

Legal duties represented

  • Declare a lawful, specific purpose and collect proportionate data.
  • Keep data accurate, retain it only as necessary, and respect data-subject rights.
  • Use risk-appropriate safeguards and confidentiality practices.

Recommended engineering controls

  • Enforce server-side object authorization for every tracking lookup.
  • Use Web Crypto random references, throttling, monitoring, and audit logs.
  • Never place customer names, passwords, files, or contact details in public identifiers or insecure offline caches.

The controls are engineering recommendations that support risk reduction; they are not quoted statutory requirements and this educational artifact is not legal advice.
